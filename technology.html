Arlington County, Virginia

Technology

Eight sectors of the defense industrial base — and where Arlington is strongest

Drone software, research funding, sensing, a degree pipeline running well ahead of the target the state set for it — and, in the last twelve months, some of the largest venture rounds in the region. Arlington's technology sector is a compact one, and it has been growing quickly and in a particular direction.

The frame

Eight sectors, borrowed from the people who fund them.

The Defense Industrial Base Consortium runs an Other Transaction agreement for the Department of Defense, sponsored by its Warfighting Investments, Resourcing and Execution office and managed by Advanced Technology International. Its purpose is to reach technologies “typically reserved for commercial development” — across supply chains, industrial workforce, sustained production and scaling — and it sorts that work into eight sectors.

They are a good set of headings for a place like this, because they cover the whole span from raw material to trained engineer. Arlington contributes to every one of them, and in four it has genuine depth: uncrewed systems, microelectronics and sensing, the research funding that sets national direction, and the workforce being built to staff all of it.

ATI, which manages the consortium, keeps its Washington office at 901 North Stuart Street in Ballston — four blocks from DARPA, and a fair illustration of how tightly this county is packed.

Sea, Land, Air, Space and Unmanned Systems

[ 05 ]

Strongest sector · growing fastest

Arlington's best sector is uncrewed systems, and it is where the recent growth has concentrated.

AeroVironment runs its corporate headquarters from 241 18th Street South in Crystal City, moved from California in 2021 because, as its chief executive put it, the Washington area is “where many of our key customers are located.” The line directed from that office runs from the Puma and Raven aircraft through JUMP 20 to the tEODor and telemax ground robots and the Switchblade loitering munitions.

Auterion followed in May 2024, moving its corporate headquarters to 4250 Fairfax Drive in Ballston, and raised a $130 million Series B the following year for the autonomy software that flies other manufacturers' drones. Innovative Defense Technologies, also in Ballston, announced a $19 million expansion and 210 new jobs in May 2026. Boeing and Leonardo DRS both run from the county.

Quartermaster, also in Arlington, raised a reported $43 million Series A in May 2026 for software that coordinates uncrewed vessels as a single system — the same problem one layer down, on water.

The buyer is close by. The Defense Innovation Unit launched its Drone Dominance Program in February 2026 and its director told the House Armed Services Committee in May that it carries $1.1 billion over eighteen months, structured as four iterative “Gauntlets.”

Microelectronics, Comms and Sensors

[ 06 ]

Research direction · sensing

The national semiconductor research agenda is set from a building in Ballston.

DARPA's Microsystems Technology Office runs the Electronics Resurgence Initiative, committed at upwards of $1.5 billion over five years, whose second phase added domestic manufacturing, hardware security and supply-chain traceability to the original work on materials and design. The Office of Naval Research funds sensors, communications and power from the next block north.

Commercially, Leonardo DRS runs advanced sensing and network computing from 2345 Crystal Drive. Teledyne FLIR Defense opened a demonstration centre at 1550 Crystal Drive in September 2025 for imaging systems and chemical, biological and explosive detection. Shift5, in Rosslyn, closed a $75 million round in 2025 for onboard operational-technology data.

The fastest-growing company in this part of the county is two years old. Twenty, founded in Arlington in 2024, raised a $100 million Series B led by Accel in June 2026 at a $1 billion valuation and another $30 million from Khosla Ventures a month later at $1.2 billion — $168 million in total, for cyber-warfare software.

Workforce

[ 07 ]

Ahead of the target set for it

The degree programme built for Amazon is outrunning its own bachelor's target.

Virginia's Tech Talent Investment Program came out of the 2018 HQ2 bid and was written into state law the following year, committing participating universities to at least 25,000 additional degrees by 2039 against a state investment usually quoted at $1.1 billion. More than $550 million had been disbursed by the end of FY2025 — and on the bachelor's side the universities have produced 2,520 degrees against an interim target of 1,155, more than double.

The Arlington building for that is Fuse at Mason Square: 345,000 square feet at roughly $254 million, of which the state programme funded $90 million. It opened commercially in December 2024, took its first academic occupants in autumn 2025, and now holds George Mason's School of Computing alongside paying commercial tenants, with 44 engineering and computing faculty and 120 doctoral students in place at the October 2025 opening.

Marymount, on Glebe Road, launched a bachelor's degree in artificial intelligence in 2023 — with a required internship or research project and a course on trust, bias and social impact — and enrolled a record 2,839 undergraduates in 2025–26, up 13 per cent on the year.

Strategic and Critical Materials

[ 01 ]

Capital and price discovery

Arlington's contribution to critical minerals is money and a market.

The Office of Strategic Capital, which works from the Pentagon and so from Arlington County, made its first direct loan to MP Materials to build out critical-minerals supply. DARPA seeded the Critical Minerals Forum and runs the Open Price Exploration for National Security programme, an effort to establish honest pricing for minerals whose markets are thin and easily distorted.

The mining and stockpiling sit elsewhere in the region — the Defense Logistics Agency's Strategic Materials office is at Fort Belvoir, and the US Geological Survey's minerals centre is in Reston, both in Fairfax County.

Kinetic Capabilities

[ 02 ]

Two headquarters

Two Fortune 500 primes chose Arlington for their global headquarters within a year of each other.

Boeing announced its move from Chicago in May 2022 and files from 929 Long Bridge Drive in National Landing. RTX runs from 1000 Wilson Boulevard in Rosslyn. AeroVironment's loitering munitions business — Switchblade 300 and 600, and Blackwing — is directed from Crystal City.

These are headquarters decisions rather than production ones: the assembly lines stay in Arizona, Alabama and elsewhere. What moved to Arlington is where the company is run from, and how close that is to the customer.

The county also has early capital pointed at this sector. Lavrock Ventures, in Ballston, holds both Castelion, which builds long-range strike systems, and Aventra, whose guidance kits convert unguided munitions into precision ones. RTX Ventures, in Rosslyn, is an investor in Ursa Major, which makes solid rocket motors and hypersonic propulsion.

Energy Storage and Batteries

[ 03 ]

Loan capital · grid-scale projects

A $1.4 billion battery commitment was signed here this month.

In August 2026 the Office of Strategic Capital committed a conditional loan of $1.4 billion to Sila Nanotechnologies for silicon-carbon anodes and lithium-ion cell production, citing satellites, uncrewed aircraft and munitions among the end uses. Lightshift Energy, a utility-scale storage developer and operator, is headquartered in the county and has been putting battery portfolios into rural power agencies. Leonardo DRS carries electric power and propulsion among its product lines.

Lightshift is well capitalised for its size. Greenbacker Capital Management put $100 million behind it in March 2024 — $20 million of growth equity and $80 million against infrastructure assets — having first invested $20 million when the company was called Delorean Power. Aiga Capital Partners added a credit facility of up to $40 million in July 2025, and KeyBanc $75 million of project finance in October.

Manufacturing — Castings and Forgings

[ 04 ]

The funding office, not the foundry

Arlington is where manufacturing communities elsewhere get their funding.

The Office of Local Defense Community Cooperation works from 2231 Crystal Drive and administers the Defense Manufacturing Community Support Program, which put $110 million into twenty-three designated communities between 2020 and 2023 to build up their industrial capacity and workforce.

Heavy manufacturing has never been an Arlington industry; the county's part in this sector is the grant-making end of it, and that is a substantial one. The designations have gone to communities around the country building up exactly the casting, forging and machining capacity the sector depends on.

There is a venture line into it too. Fabri, an investment-casting foundry that describes itself as “software-defined and technology-enabled by default,” is a Lavrock Ventures portfolio company — which puts a Ballston fund inside the one sector the county has no plants for. Steel Atlas, which co-led the seed round of the Arlington-founded GenLogs, invests under the heading “Investing in Resilience” in “the software and hardware technologies re-shaping the industrial economy.”

Critical Chemicals

[ 08 ]

National representation

The specialty chemical industry is represented from Crystal Drive.

The Society of Chemical Manufacturers and Affiliates — the trade association for specialty and fine chemical producers, the constituency this sector is built around — works from 1400 Crystal Drive. Its members' plants are elsewhere; the advocacy, standards work and policy engagement happen here, which is the pattern across several of these sectors.

Capital

Who funds it.

Two kinds of money show up in Arlington technology. The first is capital headquartered here, and almost all of it sits on one street: Lavrock at 4300 Wilson Boulevard, Marlinspike at 4201, FedTech at 4401, EJF at 2107, StoneMill at 1201, Sands Capital and RTX at 1000. Two of those funds invest almost entirely in national-security technology. The second kind is capital that arrives from somewhere else — the growth and venture funds that led the county's largest rounds of the last twelve months, none of them based nearby.

Both are worth reading against the eight sectors, because a fund's portfolio is a statement about where it thinks the industrial base is going. What follows quotes each firm's own description of what it backs, and names the companies it has actually backed. The sector tags are ours: we have placed each portfolio company against the sector its product serves, and where a company does not fit neatly we say so.

The fund next door

[ VC ]

Four of the eight sectors

One Ballston fund covers half the sector list on its own.

Lavrock Ventures works from 4300 Wilson Boulevard and describes itself as “early-stage investors at the intersection of national security and frontier technologies,” approaching venture “from the perspective of the national security ecosystem, with deep roots in the intelligence community.” It is a small fund by the standards of the names further down this page. Its portfolio is not.

Castelion builds long-range strike systems and Aventra makes guidance kits that turn unguided munitions into precision ones — sector 02. PteroDynamics builds autonomous VTOL aircraft that reach places without runways, and CesiumAstro supplies full-stack space communications — sectors 05 and 06. Maybell Quantum builds the dilution refrigerators that quantum processors have to run inside — sector 06 again.

The one that matters most for this page is Fabri. It is not a software company selling into foundries; it is a foundry, making investment castings and describing itself as “a foundry that is software-defined and technology-enabled by default.” Sector 04, castings and forgings, is the sector Arlington has no plants for. It has the investor.

The pattern

Arlington companies raise at the front of the national market.

Count the leads on the rounds above. Accel, Khosla, Bain Capital, Bessemer, Insight, Battery Ventures, First Round, Hedosophia, Greenbacker — the firms writing the largest cheques into Arlington are the firms writing the largest cheques anywhere, and none of them is based in the Washington region. Two of the syndicate names are close: Booz Allen Ventures in McLean, and Squadra Ventures, which works from Baltimore and from G Street in Washington. First In, which came into Shift5's Series A, publishes no office address at all.

That is a strong position rather than a weak one. A cluster funded only by its neighbours has a ceiling; these companies are competing for capital nationally and winning it, twice in the case of Twenty, which raised again a month after closing a round. The funds that are here — Lavrock and Marlinspike in Ballston, RTX Ventures and StoneMill in Rosslyn — are the early money, which is the money that decides which companies exist at all.

And one instrument sits here in a way it sits nowhere else. The Office of Strategic Capital works from the Pentagon, and the $25 million inside Auterion's Series B came through it. It is the same office behind the first loan to MP Materials in sector 01 and the $1.4 billion battery commitment in sector 03. Federal capital placed alongside private venture money in a single round is a recent invention, and Arlington is where it is administered from.

The short answer

What is Arlington actually good at?

Software for things that move themselves, the research funding that sets national direction, sensing and onboard data, and a university pipeline that is currently beating the target the state set for it. Those four are where the county has both institutions and momentum, and three of the four have added capital or jobs in the last eighteen months.

The growing part is easy to name: software sold to a defense or logistics customer. Autonomy, cyber, supply-chain analytics, onboard data, fleet coordination — that is where the large rounds went. Two of those companies crossed a billion-dollar valuation inside the last ten months, and one of them is two years old.

The shape of it follows from the geography. Twenty-six square miles wrapped around a Metro system, three miles from the Pentagon, is a good place to run a company, fund a programme or teach a graduate seminar, and a poor one to site a forge. Arlington has specialised accordingly, and the specialisation is paying: two Fortune 500 headquarters, two companies past a billion-dollar valuation, an autonomy company that crossed the Atlantic to be here, a computing building whose commercial space was about three-quarters committed by the time it opened, and a Ballston fund whose portfolio reaches into four of the eight sectors, including the one the county has no plants for.

Getting your bearings

The region shares a labour market and splits across four jurisdictions.

Northern Virginia's technology institutions sit close together and are easy to mix up, so it is worth knowing which is where. Virginia Tech's Innovation Campus — the graduate school that anchored the state's side of the Amazon deal, whose first academic building opened in January 2025 — is in the City of Alexandria at Potomac Yard. Both sides of that boundary market themselves as National Landing, which is where the confusion starts.

Virginia Tech also holds Arlington ground: the Research Center at 900 North Glebe Road in Ballston, 144,000 square feet, open since 2011. This guide has never stopped at the county line, and the people who work in these buildings do not either.
